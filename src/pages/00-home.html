How it works

From one measurement to every decision.

Today a chip is characterized once, at the factory, against the worst chip that could have been made, and then run that way for life. Fluid Silicon adds a small health layer beside your design so each chip measures its own logic, at speed, for as long as it runs.

{{icon:monitor}}

Measured on the chip

Timing slack on individual LUTs, carry blocks and the links between them, while the design computes. A full sweep takes about a second; each link is resolved to under 20 ps.

{{icon:model}}

Modeled per chip

An aging curve fitted to each chip's own temperature history, and a time-to-threshold for every element on it.

{{icon:tune}}

Tuned to what it measured

Voltage and frequency set to what each chip can sustain, in windows you schedule, with every change re-measured.

{{icon:repair}}

Repaired only when needed

Nothing in your design moves until an element degrades toward your threshold. Then only that path moves to a healthy resource, through your change process.

Where this goes

FPGAs are where we start.

A chip that reports its own condition is useful wherever silicon has to be trusted. FPGAs are programmable, so the health layer can be added to chips already in service, with no new silicon. That makes them the first step, not the last.

1

Today: FPGAs, read-only

Per-element measurement on AMD and Altera parts, with evaluations that start on a few of your boards.

2

Next: the full loop on every chip

Each chip monitored, modeled, tuned and, when needed, repaired on its own measurement, wherever it runs: on a bench, in a vehicle, in a cabinet or in a data center.

3

After that: beyond FPGAs

The same per-element measurement for other chips that have to run for years, so no system fails because a part ran out of margin unnoticed.

Who we are
